Chapter 7. Two Views of the Four-Day Week

In the spring of 2021 the town of Halden let 64 of its offices try a four-day week for six months. When the trial ended, two people who had watched it closely sat down with me to talk about what it meant. They did not agree on much, and I will not pretend to settle it for them.

Ingrid Moe, an economist who advised the town council, is sure the trial worked. "Output per hour rose in almost every office," she told me. "People did in four days what they used to do in five, and sick days fell by a third. If you want proof that long weeks waste time, this is it."

Paul Asante, who runs a small accounting firm that took part, sees it the other way. "Of course output per hour went up," he said. "We cancelled every meeting we could and pushed the hard work into the next quarter. Six months is not long enough to see the bill. Ask me again after tax season."

Moe answers that the offices were checked by an outside firm and that the work pushed later was small. Asante answers that the outside firm counted tasks finished, not how well they were done, and that his clients noticed slower replies. Neither of them has the numbers that would prove the other wrong.

They agree on one point. The people who took part wanted to keep the shorter week: in a survey at the end, 9 in 10 said so. Moe sees this as the strongest result of the trial. Asante sees it as the least surprising one, since few people would vote for more work.

Who is right? The trial cannot say. It lasted six months, it covered offices only, and the town did not compare them with offices that kept the five-day week. What it does show is that a shorter week could be run for half a year without the offices closing. Whether it pays for itself is a question the next trial will have to answer, and Halden has not yet decided whether to run one.
