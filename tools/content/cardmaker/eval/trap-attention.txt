Chapter 4. The Myth of the Busy Mind

As we saw in the last chapter, Marta Ruiz spent eleven years running the sleep laboratory at Northfield before she turned to the question that made her name. She was not the first to ask it. In the 1990s the management writer Dale Hobbs had told a generation of office workers that the brain is a muscle that grows stronger the more tasks it carries at once, and that people who cannot juggle five things are simply out of training. His seminars were full. His claim was never tested.

Ruiz tested it. In 2009 she and her colleague Tomas Lind recruited 212 adult office workers and asked each of them to do two ordinary jobs: proofread a page of text and answer simple sums read aloud. Half did the jobs one after the other. The other half did both at once. The second group took 40 percent longer to finish and made about twice as many errors. What surprised her was something else. When asked afterwards, the people who had done both at once rated their own performance higher than the people who had worked on one job at a time.

This is the heart of the matter. It is not that people cannot switch between jobs; they can, and quickly. It is that every switch has a price, paid in seconds and in mistakes, and that the person paying it does not feel the bill. Ruiz called this the switch cost. She was careful to say that her result came from desk work done by adults in a single afternoon, and that she had not tested drivers, surgeons or children.

Lind, for his part, never fully agreed with her reading. He thought the extra errors came mostly from the sums being read too fast, and he said so in print. The two stopped working together in 2011.

There is an old line, usually given to the Roman writer Publilius Syrus, that fits the finding well: "To do two things at once is to do neither." Whether he wrote it or not, the experiment gives it a number.

None of this means that a quiet desk is always best. They found, in a smaller follow-up with 48 people, that soft background music made no measurable difference to speed or errors. It was the second job, not the noise, that did the damage. In the next chapter we turn to what happens when the second job is a telephone.
