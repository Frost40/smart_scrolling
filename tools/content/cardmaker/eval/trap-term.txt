Chapter 6. Why the Bridge Was Late

By the time the Harbour Bridge repairs began in 2016, Adaeze Okonkwo had already spent ten years arguing for a bigger slack budget, and the repairs became her favourite example. She followed them from the first day to the last.

The plan gave the crews a slack budget of 5 percent. Within the first month, a storm closed the site for nine days and a supplier delivered the wrong steel. Each surprise ate into the slack budget, and by the end of the second month it was gone. From then on, every new problem pushed the finish date back by its full length.

Okonkwo's point was not that the planners were careless. Their estimates for each task were good: most tasks finished within a day of plan. The trouble was that the plan treated a surprise as unlikely, when on a site that size some surprise is close to certain. A slack budget of 5 percent, she wrote, is a promise that nothing will go wrong.

A second crew, working on the eastern ramp at the same time, had a slack budget of 20 percent. It met the same storm and two problems of its own, and it still finished 3 days early. Okonkwo admitted that the two crews were not a fair test of her idea, because the eastern ramp was the simpler job.

Her critics said that a large slack budget simply invites people to work slowly, since time that is set aside tends to get used. Okonkwo replied that this happens when the slack budget is shared out to every task in advance, and not when it is held back by the project leader and spent only on surprises.

The repairs finished 14 months late. The report that followed did not blame the storm or the steel. It blamed the slack budget, and it asked that the next repair plan keep a larger one, held back for surprises in the way Okonkwo had described.
