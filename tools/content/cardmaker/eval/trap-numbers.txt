Chapter 3. What the Sleep Study Found

In 2018 the Lindqvist Institute followed 1,200 students at 4 universities for one full term to see how sleep was linked to their exam results. The students wore a small band on the wrist that recorded when they slept, and they reported their marks at the end of term.

Students who slept at least 7 hours on the night before an exam scored 14 percent higher on that exam than students who slept less than 5 hours. The gap held only for exams that tested problem solving. On exams that tested memory of facts, it was too small to measure.

Over the whole term the picture was different. Students who slept at least 7 hours on most nights were 41 percent less likely to fail a course than students who slept less than 6 hours on most nights. This held for first-year students. Among older students the difference was smaller, and the team could not rule out chance.

Catching up at weekends did not help much. Students who slept less than 6 hours on weeknights and more than 9 hours at weekends did only 3 percent better than those who stayed short all week, and only in the first month of term. After that the difference disappeared.

The study had limits that the team stated plainly. All 1,200 students were volunteers, so they may have cared more about sleep than most students do. The bands measured time asleep, not how deep the sleep was. The study followed one term only, so it says nothing about sleep over a whole degree. And because the students chose how long to sleep, the study shows a link between sleep and marks, not that sleep alone caused the better marks.

Lena Okafor, who led the study, said the results were a good reason for universities to stop setting problem-solving exams at eight in the morning. She did not claim that sleep decides marks on its own, and she warned that a student who sleeps well but does not study should expect no miracle.
